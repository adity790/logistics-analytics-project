## Week 2: Data Collection, Cleaning, and Preprocessing for Logistics Analysis

This report documents a complete, reproducible data preparation pipeline for a pan-India e-commerce logistics network. I simulated a 12,160-row shipment feed whose fields mirror public datasets such as DataCo Smart Supply Chain and Olist Brazilian E-Commerce, and I deliberately included the defects found in real extracts: 160 duplicate shipment IDs, about 2,700 missing cells, nine spellings of three shipping modes, three different date formats, negative costs, zero distances and extreme weights and costs. The report explains each cleaning step, why the technique was chosen and how it affects later analysis. Duplicates and labels were standardised, dates were parsed with explicit formats, and impossible values were handled by business rules, with the target variable never imputed. Three outlier methods (IQR, z-score and a robust median/MAD rule on the log scale within product category) were compared, and the robust rule was chosen because the other two wrongly flag normal heavy parcels. Missing values were analysed with a chi-square test and five imputation methods were benchmarked by masking known weights; a volume-times-density rule was selected for its accuracy and explainability. I also compared six normalisation approaches and showed that a log transform is needed before scaling skewed variables. The report includes pandas code taken directly from the project script, a full cleaning log, and a before/after scorecard showing completeness rising from 98.8% to 99.8% with no duplicates or invalid values left. It closes with a reflection on how data quality changes KPIs, models and operational decisions in logistics. The full code, data and figures are in the GitHub repository.

## Week 3: Advanced Data Analysis and Visualization in Logistics

This report presents an exploratory analysis of 11,880 cleaned shipments from a simulated pan-India logistics network, covering delivery time, shipment volume, transport cost, weather, traffic and customer rating. It starts with descriptive statistics (mean, median, spread, skewness) and explains why medians and log scales are appropriate for skewed variables. It then uses distributions, a Spearman correlation heatmap, a monthly volume and lateness chart, a carrier-by-zone heatmap, cost-driver scatter and box plots, a ranked bar chart of bottleneck lanes, quartile charts for weather and traffic, and a rating-versus-lateness chart. Every visualisation type is justified, and every chart is followed by an interpretation tied to operations. The main findings are that the North-East zone has 12% of shipments but 19% of all late deliveries, with all five worst lanes ending in Guwahati; the October to December peak raises the late rate from 25.2% to 37.6%, a statistically significant change; distance is the dominant cost driver while Express costs about twice as much per km as Economy with little reliability gain; and customer ratings fall sharply once parcels are two or more days late. Statistical tests (two-proportion z-test, chi-square, Kruskal-Wallis) support the claims. The report includes the Python code used for the statistics and charts, a table of findings with specific recommendations such as sourcing Guwahati from Kolkata and restricting Express, and a discussion of limitations. All code, data and figures are in the GitHub repository.

## Week 4: Predictive Modeling and Optimization in Logistics Systems

This report builds and evaluates predictive and optimisation models for the logistics network. The prediction target is delivery time in days, using only features known at dispatch to avoid leakage. I compared a mean predictor, the current promised-days rule, linear and ridge regression, a decision tree, a random forest and gradient boosting, using 5-fold cross-validation inside scikit-learn pipelines, randomised hyper-parameter search, a hold-out test set and a forward-chaining time-series check. The tuned gradient-boosting model reaches RMSE 0.55 days, MAE 0.38 and R-squared 0.90, compared with RMSE 0.67 for the current rule; I note honestly that linear regression is already strong, so the gain is real but modest. A late-delivery classifier (AUC 0.76) shows that reviewing the riskiest 20% of shipments catches about 40% of late parcels. For optimisation, a transportation linear programme reallocates hub demand across warehouses under capacity, service-protection and lane-stability constraints, giving up to 19% lower delivery cost at an unchanged late rate as a planning estimate. A second linear programme tunes carrier mix by zone, and quantile models set delivery promises that reach 90% on-time with a shorter average promise than a flat buffer. The report includes the code, metric definitions, feature importance, residual analysis, caveats and prioritised recommendations. All code, data and figures are in the GitHub repository.
